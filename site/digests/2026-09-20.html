SSA / satellite-maneuver ML briefing — 14–20 September 2026

This week produced no strong new paper directly on orbital-maneuver detection comparable to MAD-LEO last week. The worthwhile material is instead methodological. Two papers are particularly relevant to your current residual-based approach: GSLAD, because it asks whether anomalies are more visible as changes in inter-variable structure than as forecast residuals, and Jensen et al., because it addresses adaptive thresholding under distribution shift without immediately contaminating the nominal reference.

4. SOTER: A Generative Time-Series Foundation Model for Wearable Human Physiological Signals

Fangke Chen, Sirry Chen, Wei Chen, Zhongyu Wei 15 September 2026 — arXiv preprint.

Direct paper — arXiv Public implementation

SOTER is not an anomaly detector, but it addresses three properties that general-purpose models such as TimesFM handle poorly for your problem:

Architecture

The model combines:

  • 1.
    continuous-time rotary positional encoding;
  • 2.
    five lower channel-independent temporal layers;
  • 3.
    one high-level cross-channel layer;
  • 4.
    power-spectral-density-guided mixture-of-experts;
  • 5.
    a Neural Controlled Differential Equation / Neural Ordinary Differential Equation decoder.

The timestamp itself, rather than the sequence index, controls positional encoding. The decoder can therefore be queried at arbitrary future times. arXiv

This is highly relevant to radar/laser SSA data where

is neither constant nor necessarily small.

Data and evaluation

SOTER is pre-trained on 226 billion physiological time points from five public datasets, then evaluated without retraining the backbone on six out-of-distribution datasets. arXiv

The authors evaluate three distinct capabilities:

  • •
    zero-shot forecasting;
  • •
    frozen-representation classification;
  • •
    continuous-time imputation.

It obtains the lowest RMSE on 4/6 zero-shot forecasting datasets, lowest MAE on 5/6, and lowest imputation error on all six datasets when 75% of observations are missing. arXiv

They also explicitly corrupt observations with additive acquisition noise; SOTER under their strongest corruption reportedly matches or exceeds several competing models operating on clean signals. arXiv +1

Reproducibility

Unlike many new foundation-model papers, source code is already public. arXiv

What this does not prove

There is no maneuver detection, no anomaly FAR, no detection latency and no anomaly-score calibration.

It therefore provides architecture evidence, not anomaly-detection evidence.

Why it matters

This is the most interesting new architecture to compare conceptually with TimesFM/TSMixer for your actual data characteristics.

Rather than regularizing radar/laser observations onto a fixed grid, test:

directly.

Then ask whether continuous-time forecasting produces:

  • 1.
    lower nominal residual variance;
  • 2.
    better calibrated uncertainty versus sampling gap;
  • 3.
    higher maneuver separability.

The third criterion is the important one. Better RMSE alone is insufficient.

5. QUALS: Corpus Equilibrium for Universal Forecasting via Pattern Quantization and Learnability Synchronization

Yujie Li, Zezhi Shao, Chengqing Yu, Yisong Fu, Weijie Zhu, Yifan Du, Jilin Hu, Bin Yang, Yongjun Xu, Fei Wang 17 September 2026 — arXiv preprint. arXiv

Direct paper — arXiv

This is relevant because it shifts attention away from architecture and toward the distribution of temporal patterns seen during pre-training.

Core idea

Large time-series corpora are heavily imbalanced. Common easy motifs dominate optimization while uncommon dynamics receive relatively little effective training.

QUALS first quantizes temporal patterns using vector quantization and uniform binning, then modifies sampling weights according to their observed learnability.

The objective is essentially to prevent:

Experiments I would add now

The highest-value experiment this week is a structural-score ablation on the model you already have. Do not implement all of GSLAD initially. Extract a time-dependent measure of cross-variable structure from nominal windows and test whether maneuver windows move away from it. Compare

using MAD-LEO or your own maneuver truth. Evaluate PR-AUC first, then freeze the scoring method and evaluate operational thresholds.

Second, change latency reporting to an empirical detection CDF:

Report it at several fixed false-alarm budgets and stratify by maneuver magnitude. SlipSense is a good example of how to make the result immediately interpretable. arXiv

Third, test gated calibration adaptation. Use two thresholds:

Scores below the first update the nominal distribution; scores between them freeze adaptation; scores above the second trigger an alarm. This directly tests whether your detector can follow slow nominal drift without learning away a maneuver. arXiv +1

Fourth, perform an irregular-sampling stress test. Starting from the same trajectories, progressively remove observations and introduce realistic radar/laser gaps. Compare your current fixed-grid preprocessing against a continuous-time model or, more simply, add explicitly to the forecasting model. Plot

at constant FAR. SOTER's results under 75% missingness make this a worthwhile architecture direction, but they do not yet establish that it helps anomaly detection. arXiv

Finally, I would add training-regime balancing as a cheap ablation before changing the backbone. If rare orbital regimes currently contribute few training windows, balance them and check whether the worst-regime nominal residual variance decreases. This may improve cross-object generalization without increasing model complexity.

The main conclusion this week is therefore fairly specific: the most promising direction is no longer simply improving the forecast. It is testing whether maneuver information appears in changes of multivariate structure that a good forecaster can otherwise absorb. GSLAD provides the clearest new experimental hypothesis for that.
